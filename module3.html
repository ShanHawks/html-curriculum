> Module 03 / 06 · ~3–5 WEEKS

Words, Tokens, Numbers & Embeddings

How a computer that only understands numbers can work with human language.

Approximately three to five weeks, depending on the instructor's campus pacing calendar. Part of Deep Learning or Delulu: Why Large Language Models Make Stuff Up — a six-module curriculum for secondary students in grades 6–12.

WEEKS: 01 02 03 04 05
Week 01

Why Computers Can't Read

Students confront the core problem every LLM has to solve — computers only work with numbers, but humans communicate in words — and learn the historical attempts to bridge that gap.

What students learn

  1. The framing question — how do you teach a machine to understand the word "cat"?
  2. ASCII and Unicode — assigning every character a number.
  3. Bag-of-words from the 1950s to 2000s — counting word frequency.
  4. The limitations of bag-of-words — it loses both order and meaning.
  5. Why this matters — every modern model still starts by turning text into numbers.
  6. Begin the wall chart, "How a Word Becomes a Number."

Key resources

  • Anil Ananthaswamy, Why Machines Learn — sections on representing data as numbers.
  • CGP Grey — How Computers Translate Human Language.
  • Computerphile — Characters, Symbols and the Unicode Miracle.
  • ASCII table reference.
  • Teacher-prepared Google Colab notebook — a simple bag-of-words counter.
  • Hello World magazine, Raspberry Pi Foundation.
Week 02

Tokens

Students learn that LLMs don't read words — they read tokens, the model's own peculiar chunks of language — and see firsthand how that choice shapes everything the model does.

What students learn

  1. The tokenization problem — there are too many possible words for a model to learn one number per word.
  2. Subword tokens — common words become one token, rare words become several.
  3. Hands-on exploration with the OpenAI tokenizer.
  4. Three discoveries — spaces belong to the next token, common English is cheap, other languages are expensive.
  5. The fairness consequence — non-English speakers pay more and get worse results.
  6. Byte-Pair Encoding, BPE, taught conceptually as the algorithm behind tokenization.
  7. Update the wall chart — text becomes tokens.

Key resources

  • OpenAI Tokenizer.
  • Tiktokenizer.
  • Andrej Karpathy — Let's build the GPT Tokenizer.
  • Simon Willison's blog.
  • Hugging Face — Summary of the tokenizers.
  • Teacher-prepared "Token Math" handout.
Week 03

From Tokens to Vectors

Students take the leap from "every token gets a number" to "every token gets a list of numbers that captures what it means" — the embedding idea that makes modern AI possible.

What students learn

  1. The leap — a single number per token carries no meaning, but a list of numbers, a vector, can.
  2. The classroom analogy — describing a person with one number versus a list of numbers.
  3. Embeddings defined — a list of numbers, often 768 or more, where similar words land near each other.
  4. The classic demonstration — king minus man plus woman is near queen.
  5. Word2Vec, 2013, Tomáš Mikolov and the Google team.
  6. Visualizing high-dimensional embeddings in 2D and 3D.
  7. The big idea — meaning is geometric. The model stores positions in space, not definitions.
  8. Update the wall chart — tokens become vectors.

Key resources

  • TensorFlow Embedding Projector.
  • 3Blue1Brown — But what is a GPT? — the embedding section.
  • Anil Ananthaswamy, Why Machines Learn — chapters on vectors.
  • Jay Alammar — The Illustrated Word2Vec.
  • Google Machine Learning Crash Course — Embeddings.
  • Teacher-prepared Colab notebook for cosine similarity exploration.
Week 04

Geometry of Meaning

Students dig into the geometry — how "close" two ideas are in vector space, what that closeness predicts well, and where it breaks down.

What students learn

  1. Cosine similarity, taught visually as the angle between two arrows.
  2. Hands-on semantic search over a small database of sentences.
  3. What embeddings capture well — synonyms, categories, analogies.
  4. Where embeddings fail — bias bakes in geometrically. "Doctor" sits closer to "he," "nurse" closer to "she."
  5. Bolukbasi and others, 2016 — the paper that named the geometric bias problem.
  6. Polysemy — the word "bank" gets one vector for two different meanings.
  7. Real-world uses — semantic search, recommendation systems, and retrieval-augmented generation.
  8. Update the wall chart — vectors become meaning through similarity and distance.

Key resources

  • TensorFlow Embedding Projector — continued from Week 3.
  • Bolukbasi and others, "Man is to Computer Programmer as Woman is to Homemaker?" (2016).
  • Jay Alammar — The Illustrated Transformer.
  • Cohere — What Are Embeddings?.
  • Simon Willison — Embeddings: What they are and why they matter.
  • Teacher-prepared semantic search Colab notebook.
Week 05

Build a Mini Semantic Search Engine

Students consolidate the whole pipeline — text to tokens to vectors to meaning — by building a small but real semantic search tool, then defend their understanding in a written and oral assessment.

What students learn

  1. Review and trace the pipeline end-to-end on the wall chart.
  2. Build a mini semantic search engine over a corpus of their choice.
  3. Explain how text became tokens.
  4. Explain how tokens became vectors.
  5. Explain why some searches worked and some didn't.
  6. Identify one thing the embedding got wrong, with a hypothesis for why.
  7. Demo day with 3 to 5 minute presentations and structured peer questions.
  8. Module 3 written assessment — vocabulary, diagram completion, and short answer.
  9. Bridge to Module 4.

Key resources

  • Google Colab.
  • Hugging Face sentence-transformers library.
  • The completed wall chart from Weeks 1 to 4.
  • 3Blue1Brown GPT series — re-watched as review.
  • Teacher-provided rubric and sample student-built search engines.
Synthesis

Interactive Review — Predict the Token Count

Before you check, write down your prediction for how many tokens each phrase will use. Then test it yourself on the OpenAI Tokenizer and see how close you were.

Phrase 1

the cat sat on the mat


Show the answer

Around 6 or 7 tokens. All common English words, mostly one token each.

Phrase 2

antidisestablishmentarianism


Show the answer

Around 6 to 8 tokens. It's one word, but the model breaks it into subword pieces because the whole word is rare.

Phrase 3

¡Hola! ¿Cómo estás?


Show the answer

Around 10 to 12 tokens. Non-English languages and special characters like ¡ and ¿ cost significantly more tokens than English text. This is why non-English speakers effectively pay more to use the same models — the same idea from Week 2's fairness discussion.

Phrase 4

🐱🐶🦊🐰


Show the answer

Around 8 to 16 tokens depending on the model. Emoji are often multiple tokens each, even though they look like a single character.

Reflection

Which of the four phrases was most expensive in tokens? What does that tell you about how LLMs “see” different languages?
