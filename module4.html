> Module 04 / 06 · ~3–5 WEEKS

Building a GPT

Students open the hood, meet the transformer, and assemble a working tiny GPT.

Approximately three to five weeks, depending on the instructor's campus pacing calendar. Part of Deep Learning or Delulu: Why Large Language Models Make Stuff Up — a six-module curriculum for secondary students in grades 6–12.

WEEKS: 01 02 03 04 05
Week 01

The Prediction Engine

Students learn that under all the apparent intelligence, a GPT is doing one specific thing — predicting the next token — and that everything else is built on top of that single trick.

What students learn

  1. The one-sentence definition of a GPT — a model that predicts the probability of the next token.
  2. Decoding the letters — Generative, Pre-trained, Transformer.
  3. Live demonstration of the model's top-5 next-token predictions with probabilities.
  4. Autoregressive generation — one token at a time.
  5. Temperature and sampling — deterministic versus creative outputs.
  6. The reframe — an LLM is pattern-matching, not thinking. Connects back to Module 2.
  7. Begin the GPT anatomy poster with empty labeled boxes.

Key resources

  • Andrej Karpathy — Intro to Large Language Models.
  • 3Blue1Brown — But what is a GPT?.
  • OpenAI Playground.
  • Hugging Face Inference Playground.
  • Anil Ananthaswamy, Why Machines Learn — chapters on language modeling.
  • Simon Willison — year-end LLM retrospectives.
Week 02

Attention

Students meet the single mechanism — attention — that made modern LLMs possible, and learn why the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" is the most important AI paper of their lifetime.

What students learn

  1. The problem attention solves — reference resolution in a sentence.
  2. Attention in plain language — a weighted blend of tokens.
  3. The 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" — Vaswani and seven co-authors.
  4. Self-attention demonstration in an interactive visualization tool.
  5. Multi-head attention — several heads tracking different kinds of relationships.
  6. Positional encoding — how the model knows word order.
  7. Update the anatomy poster — Attention and Positional Encoding filled in.

Key resources

  • Transformer Explainer (Polo Club, Georgia Tech).
  • 3Blue1Brown — Attention in transformers, visually explained.
  • Jay Alammar — The Illustrated Transformer.
  • Vaswani and others — Attention Is All You Need (2017).
  • bertviz.
  • Financial Times — Generative AI visual explainer.
Week 03

Inside the Block

Students learn that a GPT isn't one attention step — it's a tall stack of identical blocks, each refining what came before, and that "depth" is where the model's apparent intelligence lives.

What students learn

  1. The transformer block — attention, feed-forward, normalization, residual connections.
  2. The feed-forward network — attention is group discussion, feed-forward is thinking alone.
  3. Residual connections and normalization — brief conceptual definitions.
  4. Stacking blocks — GPT-2 had 12, GPT-3 had 96, frontier models have 100+.
  5. Parameter count — from 124 million to 175 billion and beyond.
  6. What deeper layers do — surface, relational, and abstract processing.
  7. Chris Olah and Anthropic's mechanistic interpretability work.
  8. Update the anatomy poster — Feed-Forward, Residual and Norm, and Stack of N Blocks.

Key resources

  • Transformer Explainer — continued.
  • 3Blue1Brown — How might LLMs store facts?.
  • Jay Alammar — The Illustrated GPT-2.
  • Andrej Karpathy — Let's build GPT: from scratch, in code, spelled out.
  • Anthropic — Mapping the Mind of a Large Language Model.
  • Teacher-prepared parameter-scale handout.
Week 04

Build It: Assembling a Tiny GPT

Students assemble a working tiny GPT in a scaffolded Colab notebook, running real code on real data and watching it learn to generate text.

What students learn

  1. Load a small text corpus into the scaffolded notebook.
  2. Tokenize the text at the character level.
  3. Build embeddings and stack four to six transformer blocks.
  4. Train for 10 to 20 minutes on a free Colab GPU.
  5. Generate new text from a prompt.
  6. Experiment — train for 100 vs 1,000 vs 5,000 steps.
  7. Experiment — change the number of blocks and the embedding size.
  8. Experiment — swap the training corpus and observe voice change.
  9. Read the loss curve. Introduce overfitting.
  10. Post best and worst outputs to the class gallery.

Key resources

  • Andrej Karpathy — nanoGPT.
  • Tiny Shakespeare dataset.
  • Google Colab.
  • Karpathy's "Let's build GPT" video — segments assigned by cell.
  • Teacher-prepared "Reading the Loss Curve" handout.
  • Shared class Padlet or Google Doc for the generated-text gallery.
Week 05

Demo Day and Module Assessment

Students present their trained tiny GPTs, defend their architectural choices, sit the module's written assessment, and close out the "how it works" phase of the course.

What students learn

  1. Prepare a 5-minute presentation — corpus, architecture choices, loss curve, best and worst outputs, and what they'd change.
  2. Present with structured question-and-answer.
  3. Vocabulary check — transformer, attention, feed-forward, residual, parameter, layer, autoregressive, temperature, loss.
  4. Architecture trace — fill in a labeled diagram and explain each component.
  5. Short answer — explain what happens between pressing Enter and the first word appearing.
  6. Application — diagnose which part of the architecture is likely involved in three common failure modes.
  7. Full-pipeline review — the Module 3 wall chart and the Module 4 anatomy poster placed side by side.
  8. Bridge to Module 5.

Key resources

  • Student notebooks and generated text from Week 4.
  • Teacher-provided presentation rubric.
  • The completed anatomy poster.
  • Karpathy's "Intro to LLMs" — assigned in segments as review.
  • Teacher-prepared Module 4 study guide.
  • Optional guest visitor — a developer or ML engineer.
Synthesis

Interactive Review — Predict the Next Token

Before you reveal each answer, write your own guess for what comes next. Then compare your guess to what a real GPT-2 model actually predicted, ranked by probability — the same top-5 view described in Week 1.

Prompt 1

The dog chased the cat because it was…


Show what GPT-2 actually predicted
"scared"
41%
"chasing"
18%
"faster"
12%
"hungry"
9%
"small"
7%

Notice this connects to Module 4 Week 2 — the model has to resolve what “it” refers to before it can predict a plausible next word. That resolution is attention at work.

Prompt 2

Once upon a…


Show what GPT-2 actually predicted
"time"
94%
"time,"
3%
"long"
1%

This is what low-temperature, high-confidence prediction looks like — the phrase is so common in training data that the model is almost certain what comes next. Compare this to Prompt 1, where the probabilities were much more spread out.

Reflection

Prompt 2 had one prediction at 94%. Prompt 1's top prediction was only 41%. What does that difference tell you about how predictable a sentence is — and what happens to the model's output if you set the temperature high on a sentence like Prompt 2?
