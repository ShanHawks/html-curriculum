> Module 06 / 06 · ~3–5 WEEKS · CAPSTONE

Present, Critique, Defend & Deploy Your Model

The capstone. Where students stop being learners and start being practitioners.

Approximately three to five weeks, depending on the instructor's campus pacing calendar. Part of Deep Learning or Delulu: Why Large Language Models Make Stuff Up — a six-module curriculum for secondary students in grades 6–12.

WEEKS: 01 02 03 04 05
Week 01

Capstone Definition

Students define their capstone project — what their model does, who it's for, and what success looks like — and learn that scoping is the most underrated skill in technical work.

What students learn

  1. The capstone deliverable defined — a working AI-powered tool with documentation, evaluation, and public presentation.
  2. Three project tracks — Build, Modify, or Evaluate. Students choose one.
  3. Scoping discipline — write a one-page project brief.
  4. Answer the five brief questions — what it does, who it's for, what problem it solves, what "working" looks like, and what's out of scope.
  5. Stakeholder mapping revisited from Module 2.
  6. Write kill criteria — what would make you stop or pivot.
  7. One-on-one project conference with the teacher.
  8. Begin the project wall — one column per student, visible weekly progress.

Key resources

  • Teacher-prepared "Project Brief" template.
  • Past student capstones from Modules 3, 4, and 5.
  • Simon Willison — "Tools I built" posts.
  • Teacher-prepared "Scope Creep" handout.
  • The completed wall artifacts from Modules 3, 4, and 5.
  • Anthropic API documentation.
  • OpenAI API documentation.
  • Hugging Face Inference API.
  • Ollama.
Week 02

The Working Week

Students do the actual technical work of their capstone with structured support, daily check-ins, and the explicit expectation that things will break and they'll have to debug.

What students learn

  1. Day 1 — debugging mindset. Ask which stage of the pipeline produced the problem.
  2. Day 2 — iteration logs. A running record of what was tried, what happened, and what was learned.
  3. Day 3 — edge cases and adversarial testing. Deliberately try to break your own tool.
  4. Day 4 — user testing with a partner. Watch the user without helping.
  5. Day 5 — pre-mortem. Write why the presentation would fail, then fix those things.
  6. Daily standups — 3 minutes per student. What I did, what I'm doing, what's blocking me.
  7. Documentation written in parallel with the work, not after.

Key resources

  • All scaffolded Colab notebooks reused from Modules 3, 4, and 5.
  • Teacher-prepared "Stuck List" with the five most common problems.
  • Shared class Slack, Discord, or Google Chat for peer help.
  • Teacher-prepared "Iteration Log" template.
  • Teacher-prepared "User Testing" handout with sentence stems.
Week 03

Critique

Students learn — explicitly, as a teachable skill — how to give technical critique that improves work, how to receive critique without flinching, and how to decide which feedback to act on.

What students learn

  1. Three modes of critique — diagnostic, generative, and adversarial.
  2. Critique sentence stems taught and practiced.
  3. The receiving side — don't defend, take notes, thank the person.
  4. Sort feedback into three piles — act now, act later, decline with a reason.
  5. The Critique Circle exercise — rotate as presenter, critic, and notetaker.
  6. Cross-track critique — Build students critique Evaluate students, and vice versa.
  7. Write a prioritized Feedback Action Plan.
  8. Connect to professional practice — this is code review and design review.

Key resources

  • Teacher-prepared Critique Circle protocol.
  • Teacher-prepared "Feedback Sorting" worksheet.
  • Structured-literacy oral language protocols adapted for technical critique.
  • Short excerpts from public design or code review sessions.
  • Talks where practitioners critique their own past work.
  • Teacher-prepared "Critique Vocabulary" reference card.
Week 04

Defend

Students prepare the presentation of their capstone — not as performance, but as the act of standing behind their choices, explaining their reasoning, and answering hard questions.

What students learn

  1. A fixed presentation structure — problem, approach, demo, what worked, what didn't, defense.
  2. The "what didn't work" section is the heart of the talk.
  3. Demo strategy — rehearse until boring, have a fallback, narrate as you go.
  4. Anticipate the 10 hardest questions and prepare 1 to 2 sentence answers.
  5. Hostile Audience practice round with invited adults.
  6. Minimal slide design — no more than 7 slides, no more than 10 words per slide.
  7. Assemble the Defense Notebook — brief, iteration log, evaluation, feedback action plan, prepared answers.

Key resources

  • Teacher-prepared presentation template.
  • Teacher-prepared "10 Hardest Questions" worksheet.
  • Hostile Audience practice round with invited adults.
  • Practitioner talks that model honest, technically-grounded presentation.
  • Teacher-prepared "Defense Notebook" assembly checklist.
  • Teacher-prepared presentation rubric aligned with prior modules.
Week 05

Deploy and Close

Students present their capstones to a real audience, reflect on the entire course, and learn what it means to put something into the world and walk away from it.

What students learn

  1. Demo Day — public presentation to parents, administrators, and partner-organization staff.
  2. Presentation, gallery walk, and closing reflection.
  3. Deployment defined honestly at student scale.
  4. Write a one-page Handoff Document for whoever will use or extend the tool.
  5. Course-wide reflection — four written questions.
  6. Assemble the closing artifact — photographs of every wall poster from Modules 1 through 6.
  7. Named next steps — high school AI courses, online resources, communities, scholarships.
  8. Final closing — each student answers aloud, "What is one specific way you will use AI differently because of this course, and why?"

Key resources

  • All capstone work — projects, iteration logs, feedback action plans, defense notebooks.
  • Teacher-prepared Handoff Document template.
  • Teacher-prepared Reflection Prompt handout.
  • Photographs of every wall artifact from Modules 1 through 6.
  • Teacher-curated "What Comes Next" pathways list.
  • Recruited Demo Day audience.
  • A short closing letter from the teacher to each student.
Synthesis

Interactive — Choose Your Capstone Track

Expand each track below before you commit. Then draft the start of your project brief — you can revise it at your one-on-one conference with the instructor.

Track 1

Build track — what does it involve?

You build a fine-tuned model or a RAG-powered tool over a corpus you care about. Examples — a study-guide generator for a specific textbook, a chatbot that speaks in the voice of a historical figure, a writing-feedback tool for younger students.

Skills built: Fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation, data curation, evaluating your own model's outputs.

Success looks like: A working Colab notebook others can run, a documented corpus, and a live demonstration of the tool doing what you designed it to do.

Best fit if you: Enjoyed building the tiny GPT in Module 4 and want to go deeper into the mechanics of training a model.

Track 2

Modify track — what does it involve?

You build a prompt-engineered application using a frontier model API (Claude, GPT-4) or a thoughtfully-configured custom GPT, aimed at a real use case with real users.

Skills built: Advanced prompt engineering, system prompt design, user testing, iteration based on real feedback.

Success looks like: A working tool with a documented prompt library, evidence that real users tested it, and a written reflection on what changed between your first version and your final one.

Best fit if you: Want to focus on building something people can actually use, and are more interested in application design than training internals.

Track 3

Evaluate track — what does it involve?

You produce a rigorous, original evaluation of an existing model for a specific task you care about — deeper than the Module 5 audit. More test cases, more models compared, a written recommendation at the end.

Skills built: Evaluation design, test-case writing, rubric development, technical writing.

Success looks like: A written report comparing 2 to 4 models on a specific task, with 15 or more test cases, a scoring rubric, and a clear, defended recommendation.

Best fit if you: Enjoyed the Module 5 model audit and want to develop the specific skill set of a machine-learning evaluator or data scientist.
