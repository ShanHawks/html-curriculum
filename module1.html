> Module 01 / 06 · ~3–5 WEEKS

Origins, History, Key Pioneers & Foundations

Where artificial intelligence came from, and the people who built it.

Approximately three to five weeks, depending on the instructor's campus pacing calendar. Part of Deep Learning or Delulu: Why Large Language Models Make Stuff Up — a six-module curriculum for secondary students in grades 6–12.

WEEKS: 01 02 03 04 05
Week 01

The Pre-History of AI (1840s to 1940s)

Students get the course map and meet the mathematicians and logicians whose work, before AI was even a word, made the field possible.

→ Open Week 1 CALP paragraph & 5-day schedule

What students learn

  1. Course overview, syllabus, and a preview of the capstone project.
  2. Working glossary — AI, machine learning, deep learning, generative AI, LLM, algorithm, neural network.
  3. Ada Lovelace, 1843 — the first published algorithm.
  4. George Boole, 1854 — true and false logic, the foundation under every computer.
  5. Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts, 1943 — the first mathematical model of an artificial neuron.
  6. Claude Shannon, 1948 — information theory, the bit, measuring meaning.
  7. Start a class timeline poster from 1843 onward.

Key resources

  • Anil Ananthaswamy, Why Machines Learn — Prologue and Chapter 1.
  • CGP Grey, How Machines Learn on YouTube — twelve minutes.
  • Computer History Museum timeline.
  • James Essinger, Ada's Algorithm.
  • Diane Stanley, Ada Lovelace, Poet of Science.
Week 02

The Birth of AI (1950 to 1969)

Students follow AI from Turing's 1950 question, "Can machines think?" through the 1956 Dartmouth workshop that named the field, into the optimistic first decade of symbolic AI.

What students learn

  1. Alan Turing, 1950 — the Imitation Game, and what counts as thinking.
  2. Grace Hopper, 1952 — the first compiler.
  3. The Dartmouth Workshop, 1956 — the field is officially named.
  4. Frank Rosenblatt, 1958 — the Perceptron, the first trainable neural network.
  5. Arthur Samuel, 1959 — coins the term "machine learning."
  6. ELIZA, 1966 — the first chatbot. Students try a recreation.
  7. Extend the class timeline.

Key resources

  • Ananthaswamy, Why Machines Learn — Chapter 2.
  • Turing's 1950 paper, opening pages, with a plain-language teacher rewrite.
  • Dartmouth 1955 proposal for the 1956 workshop — two pages, available from Stanford's archive.
  • Online ELIZA recreation.
  • Laurie Wallmark, Grace Hopper: Queen of Computer Code.
Week 03

The AI Winters (1970 to 1995)

Students learn that progress wasn't a straight line. Funding collapsed twice, and a small group of researchers kept neural networks alive against consensus until the math caught up.

What students learn

  1. The first AI Winter, 1974 — the Lighthill Report and why early neural networks stalled.
  2. Expert systems boom in the early 1980s — MYCIN and XCON.
  3. The second AI Winter in the late 1980s.
  4. Geoffrey Hinton, Yann LeCun, and Yoshua Bengio — the three who kept the faith.
  5. Backpropagation, 1986 — how a network learns from mistakes.
  6. Judea Pearl — probability and causal reasoning enter AI.
  7. Deep Blue defeats Kasparov, 1997 — the bridge to what comes next.

Key resources

  • Ananthaswamy, Why Machines Learn — Chapters 3 and 4.
  • Cade Metz, Genius Makers — Chapters 1 to 3.
  • 3Blue1Brown, "But what is a neural network?" and "Gradient descent" on YouTube.
  • Computer History Museum, Deep Blue exhibit.
Week 04

The Deep Learning Revolution (2006 to 2017)

Students see how three ingredients — bigger data, faster GPUs, and better algorithms — turned the quiet neural-network research of the winters into the technology behind every modern AI system.

What students learn

  1. 2006 — Hinton's deep belief networks paper. "Deep learning" becomes a usable term.
  2. Fei-Fei Li and ImageNet, 2009 — the dataset that made modern AI possible.
  3. AlexNet, 2012 — Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton. The proof.
  4. The role of GPUs — NVIDIA and CUDA.
  5. Ian Goodfellow, 2014 — generative adversarial networks.
  6. Demis Hassabis and DeepMind — AlphaGo defeats Lee Sedol, 2016.
  7. 2017 — "Attention Is All You Need." The Transformer is born.

Key resources

  • Ananthaswamy, Why Machines Learn — Chapters 5 to 7.
  • Metz, Genius Makers — Chapters 4 to 8.
  • Fei-Fei Li, The Worlds I See — selected excerpts; plus her TED Talk.
  • AlphaGo documentary on YouTube.
  • 3Blue1Brown, "What is a GPT?" — Part 1 only.
Week 05

From Transformers to ChatGPT (2018 to Present)

Students connect the Transformer breakthrough to the LLMs they actually use, meet the people and labs behind them, and consolidate Module 1 into a single coherent story.

What students learn

  1. The Transformer in plain language — attention as the model deciding what to focus on.
  2. GPT-1, GPT-2, GPT-3 — the scaling story. Ilya Sutskever and Alec Radford as key figures.
  3. The major labs — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta AI, Hugging Face.
  4. Timnit Gebru and Emily Bender, 2021 — the Stochastic Parrots paper introduced as historical fact.
  5. November 2022 — ChatGPT launches. AI goes mainstream.
  6. 2023 to today — Claude, Gemini, Llama, multimodal models.
  7. Module 1 synthesis project — each student profiles one pioneer.
  8. Module 1 assessment — vocabulary, timeline sequencing, and short answer.

Key resources

  • Ananthaswamy, Why Machines Learn — closing chapters and epilogue.
  • Metz, Genius Makers — final chapters.
  • 3Blue1Brown, "What is a GPT?" — full series.
  • Financial Times, "Generative AI: a visual explainer."
  • Anthropic, "Core Views on AI Safety," and OpenAI's About page.
